\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

This essay develops the dynamics observed in a rotating reference frame. We begin with the
kinematical relations required to compare time derivatives in inertial and rotating frames,
then derive the acceleration transformation and the inertial forces that follow from it.

\subsection{Kinematical Preliminaries}

Let $\vb{A}(t)$ be the orthogonal matrix that maps the components of a vector in the inertial
frame to its components in the rotating frame:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{g}'(t) = \vb{A}(t)\,\vb{g}(t),
  \qquad
  \vb{g}(t) = \vb{A}^{\top}(t)\,\vb{g}'(t).
  \label{rotating-frames:frame-transformation}
\end{equation}
The angular velocity is defined from the infinitesimal rotation vector by
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega} = \lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{d\boldsymbol{\Omega}}{\Delta t}.
  \label{rotating-frames:angular-velocity-definition}
\end{equation}
The corresponding time-derivative rule is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\vb{g}}{dt}
  = \vb{A}^{\top}(t)\!\left(
      \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{g}' + \frac{d\vb{g}'}{dt}
    \right).
  \label{rotating-frames:time-derivative-rule}
\end{equation}
These relations are the only results from rigid-body kinematics needed below.

\section{Equations of Motion in a Rotating Frame}
% ============================================================

In this section, we will thoroughly derive the equations of motion for a body in a rotating
frame of reference with respect to a fixed one. We will limit our discussion to cases of
constant angular velocity. The equations of motion will be expressed within the rotating
frame, introducing the Coriolis and centrifugal forces that emerge in these scenarios.

We start from the equation giving the rate of change of a vector in the fixed frame
\eqref{rotating-frames:time-derivative-rule}. We compute the second time derivative of the position vector
$\vb{r}$ in the fixed frame, and we consider its components in the rotating frame $\vb{r}'$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d^2\vb{r}(t)}{dt^2} =
    \frac{d\vb{A}^\top}{dt}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}' + \frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}\right)
    + \vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt} + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}\right)
\end{equation}

From the orthogonality of the rotation matrix, $\vb{A}^\top\vb{A} = \vb{I}$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\vb{A}^\top}{dt}\,\vb{A} + \vb{A}\,\frac{d\vb{A}^\top}{dt} = 0
\end{equation}
we obtain:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\vb{A}^\top}{dt} = -\vb{A}^\top\frac{d\vb{A}}{dt}\,\vb{A}^\top
\end{equation}

Inserting this expression in the equation above, and using the distributive property of the
cross product for orthogonal matrices,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \frac{d^2\vb{r}(t)}{dt^2}
    &= -\vb{A}^\top\frac{d\vb{A}}{dt}\,\vb{A}^\top
       \!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}' + \frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}\right)
       + \vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt} + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}\right) \\
    &= -\vb{A}^\top\frac{d\vb{A}}{dt}
       \!\left(\vb{A}^\top\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{A}^\top\vb{r}'
         + \vb{A}^\top\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}\right)
       + \vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt} + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}\right) \\
    &= -\vb{A}^\top\!\left(\vb{A}\!\left(\vb{A}^\top\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{A}^\top\vb{r}'
         + \vb{A}^\top\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}\right)\right)\times\boldsymbol{\omega}
       + \vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt} + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}\right) \\
    &= -\vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}' + \frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}\right)\times\boldsymbol{\omega}
       + \vb{A}^\top\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt} + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}\right)
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

we arrive at the equation:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{A}\,\frac{d^2\vb{r}(t)}{dt^2} =
    \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}')
    + 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}
    + \frac{d^2\vb{r}'}{dt^2}.
\end{equation}

Defining the acceleration in the fixed frame as $\vb{a} = \frac{d^2\vb{r}(t)}{dt^2}$, and
the acceleration in the rotating frame as $\vb{a}' = \frac{d^2\vb{r}'(t)}{dt^2}$, we obtain
the equation of motion:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{a}' = -\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}')
    - 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}
    + \vb{A}\,\vb{a}
  \label{rotating-frames:acceleration-transformation}
\end{equation}

The centrifugal force $\vb{F}_\text{cf}$ on a body of mass $m$ is the term
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_\text{cf} = -m\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}')
    = -m\bigl(\boldsymbol{\omega}\ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\vb{r}'} - \omega^2\vb{r}'\bigr)
  \label{rotating-frames:centrifugal-force}
\end{equation}
which has a magnitude proportional to the square of the angular velocity, and is directed
radially outward from the axis of rotation. The Coriolis force is the term dependent on the
body velocity,
\begin{equation}
  F_\text{cor} = -2m\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}
  \label{rotating-frames:coriolis-force}
\end{equation}
which arises from the movement of the body in the rotating frame and is perpendicular to the
velocity of the body.

\subsection{Generalization to Time-Dependent Angular Velocity}

The original document assumes constant angular velocity. For completeness, we record the
standard extension needed when the angular velocity of the frame varies with time. Applying
the time-derivative rule to $\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}'$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \vb{a}' = \vb{A}\,\vb{a}
    - \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}')
    - 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\frac{d\vb{r}'}{dt}
    - \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\vb{r}'.
  \label{rotating-frames:variable-angular-velocity-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The additional term corresponds to the Euler force
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_\text{Euler}
    = -m\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\vb{r}'.
  \label{rotating-frames:euler-force}
\end{equation}
This is the only dynamical term in this essay that was not present in the original document.

\subsection{Lagrange Equations of Motion in a Rotating Frame}

In this section and the next, we will shorten the symbol of time derivative $da/dt$ to
$\dot{a}$ to simplify the equations. Further, we will assume that all quantities are in the
rotating frame, and $\vb{r}$ represents the position vector in such a frame.

The equations of motion in a rotating frame can be expressed using Lagrangian mechanics. The
Lagrangian is defined as the difference between the kinetic and potential energies of the
system:
\begin{equation}
  L = T - V
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the kinetic energy and $V$ is the potential of the forces in the system.

The centrifugal force admits a potential $V_\text{cf}$ calculated as:
\begin{equation}
  V_\text{cf} = \frac{m}{2}\bigl(-\ip{\vb{r}}{\vb{r}}\ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
    + \ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\vb{r}}^2\bigr)
\end{equation}
thus,
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_\text{cf} = -\frac{\partial V_\text{cf}}{\partial\vb{r}}
\end{equation}

The Coriolis force, similarly to the Lorentz force in electromagnetism, admits a generalized
potential $V_\text{cor}$ determined as,
\begin{equation}
  V_\text{cor} = -m\ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}}{\dot{\vb{r}}}
\end{equation}
so that the Coriolis force derives from a generalized potential:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}_\text{cor} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial V_\text{cor}}{\partial\dot{\vb{r}}}
    - \frac{\partial V_\text{cor}}{\partial\vb{r}}
\end{equation}

Consequently, the Lagrangian of the system becomes:
\begin{equation}
  L = \frac{1}{2}m\ip{\dot{\vb{r}}}{\dot{\vb{r}}}
    + \frac{m}{2}\bigl(-\ip{\vb{r}}{\vb{r}}\ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
      + \ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\vb{r}}^2\bigr)
    - m\ip{\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r}}{\vb{v}}
\end{equation}

The Lagrange equations of motion are given by:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\vb{r}}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vb{r}} = 0
\end{equation}

The first term of the Lagrangian gives:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\vb{r}}} = m\dot{\vb{r}}
\end{equation}

The second term of the Lagrangian gives:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial\vb{r}} = -m\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r})
    - 2m\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\dot{\vb{r}}
\end{equation}

The Lagrange equations of motion in a rotating frame are then:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{\vb{r}} = -\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\vb{r})
    - 2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\dot{\vb{r}}
\end{equation}

It has to be noted that a conservative force in the inertial frame is not necessarily
conservative in the rotating frame. A simple example can be easily constructed by considering
a constant force in the plane $z=0$ of the inertial frame such as:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F} = F_x\,\vb{i} + F_y\,\vb{j}
\end{equation}
where $F_x$ and $F_y$ are constants and $\vb{i}$, $\vb{j}$ are the unit vectors of the
inertial frame. The force in a frame rotating with constant angular velocity $\omega$ around
the axis $z$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{F}' =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos\omega t  & \sin\omega t & 0 \\
    -\sin\omega t & \cos\omega t & 0 \\
    0             & 0            & 1
  \end{pmatrix}
  \vb{F}
  = (F_x\cos(\omega t) + F_y\sin(\omega t))\,\vb{i}'
  + (-F_x\sin(\omega t) + F_y\cos(\omega t))\,\vb{j}'
\end{equation}
where $\vb{i}'$, $\vb{j}'$ are the unit vectors of the rotating frame. The force in the
rotating frame is time-dependent, and does not have a generalized potential as it does not
explicitly depend on the velocity. Each case will have to be considered individually to
determine whether the dynamics can be described in a Lagrangian formalism, and in general the
equations of motion \eqref{rotating-frames:acceleration-transformation} will have to be used.
